UNIT-3 Speaking and Reading
3.1 Different Types of Reading: Note Taking and Paraphrasing
A. Different Types of Reading
A reader may employ four basic types of reading skills depending on the text and purpose
,
:
1. Skimming
    ◦ Purpose: To quickly gather the most important information or the general sense ("gist") from a document or novel
.
    ◦ Method: The reader’s eyes run over the text, noting important information, key words, and concepts
.
    ◦ Application: Used when rapidly reading a newspaper to get the general news and decide which articles merit detailed reading
.
2. Scanning
    ◦ Purpose: To find a particular, specific piece of information
.
    ◦ Method: The reader’s eyes run over the text looking exclusively for the desired specific information
.
    ◦ Application: Used when looking through schedules, meeting plans, advertisements, or searching an index or table of contents
.
3. Extensive Reading
    ◦ Purpose: To obtain a general understanding of a subject
.
    ◦ Method: Involves reading longer texts, often for pleasure or to improve general knowledge, without needing to understand every word
.
    ◦ Application: Reading a novel for pleasure or reviewing business reports to understand general trends
.
4. Intensive Reading
    ◦ Purpose: To extract detailed information from shorter texts; involves very close and accurate reading for specifics
.
    ◦ Importance: It is crucial to understand every word, fact, or piece of information
. This skill is necessary for understanding concepts, theories (e.g., in a textbook), interpreting text, inferring the central idea, observing the common thread, and intuiting overall purpose
.
    ◦ Application: Reviewing a contract or insurance claim before signing
.
B. Note Taking
Note taking is the practice of writing down important information for later recall
, acting as an essential link between generating ideas, conducting research, and planning
.
• Purposes of Note Taking:
    ◦ Remembering: Helps recall important details that might otherwise be forgotten
,
.
    ◦ Understanding: The act of writing information in one's own words helps in comprehension and making sense of the content
,
.
    ◦ Reviewing: Notes serve as a resource for studying and reviewing for examinations
.
    ◦ Organizing and Planning: Notes help organize information for easy retrieval
and are essential for planning longer texts,
.
    ◦ Generating Ideas: Notes can be used as a means of generating ideas, often via 'brainstorming on paper'
.
• Note-Making Formats: There are various formats, including freeform (collects ideas quickly and shows links)
,, list (useful for generating a plan hierarchy),, table (best for recording certain types of information),, flowchart (used for recording processes),, and concept map or mind map,,,. Concept maps are highly flexible and start with a central idea branching out to related ideas,
.
• Tips for Effective Note-Taking:
    ◦ Clarity and Simplicity: Write in your own words using simple language and avoid long sentences
.
    ◦ Speed: Use abbreviations or shorten long words to write faster
.
    ◦ Visual Aids: Draw simple pictures or symbols to represent ideas if words are difficult
.
    ◦ Structure/Emphasis: Use different colors for headings or important points to make information stand out
, or underline key parts/words/concepts for future reference
.
    ◦ Post-Activity: Review and rewrite unclear notes later in a way that makes sense to you
.
C. Paraphrasing
Paraphrasing means saying something in your own words, expressing the same idea using different language, without repeating exactly what someone else said
.
• Purposes of Paraphrasing:
    ◦ Understanding and Learning: Explaining information in one's own words helps to understand and remember the content better
.
    ◦ Avoiding Plagiarism: It is essential to paraphrase when using someone else's ideas to avoid copying
.
    ◦ Demonstrating Comprehension: Competent listeners employ paraphrasing techniques to show comprehension of the speaker’s message
,
.
• How to Paraphrase:
    ◦ Read or listen carefully to fully understand the main idea first
.
    ◦ Use different words to express the idea, retaining the original meaning
.
    ◦ Break down complex or difficult sentences into smaller parts and explain each part individually
.
    ◦ Avoid using the exact same words as the original source
.
3.2 Comprehension Passage
Comprehension passages require the reader to demonstrate a grasp of the written material, fundamentally relying on effective reading skills
,
.
• Basic Steps to Effective Reading and Comprehension: One of the four basic steps to effective reading involves constantly testing or assessing your comprehension during reading and when the task is completed
. This monitoring helps identify discrepancies in total comprehension
.
• Reading Methods for Deep Comprehension:
    ◦ Intensive Reading: Crucial for grasping details, interpreting the text, inferring the central idea, and intuiting the overall purpose and significance of the passage
.
    ◦ SQ3R (Survey–Question–Read–Recite–Review): This method involves "immaculate and perfect reading," enabling the reader to absorb the information and apply it without needing to refer back to notes or the book
.
    ◦ Visualization: Converting written information into a pictorial format while reading can enhance effective comprehension
.
• Overcoming Obstacles to Comprehension: Effective comprehension requires eliminating faulty reading habits
.
    ◦ Plodding: Reading word by word, which actually reduces understanding rather than increasing it
.
    ◦ Vocalization: Reading aloud or sounding out words, which significantly slows the reading rate and mars comprehension of the text
,,
.
    ◦ Regression: Habitually going back to reread a section when an idea is not understood, which suggests incompetence in reading skills
. For efficient reading, the eye should move over at least 2–3 words in one go
.
    ◦ Lack of Concentration: If the mind is preoccupied with other thoughts, the time spent reading is wasted
.
3.3 Group Discussion Strategies: Techniques for Individual Contribution in GD: Topic Analysis
A Group Discussion (GD) is a communication activity where a small group discusses a specific topic, commonly used in professional and academic selection processes to evaluate candidates
,. GD is categorized as group communication according to the number of participants,
.
• Importance of Group Discussions:
    ◦ Diverse Perspectives: Brings together individuals with different backgrounds, leading to a rich exchange of views
.
    ◦ Communication Skills: Enhances the ability to express ideas clearly and persuasively while actively listening
.
    ◦ Critical Thinking: Promotes analysis, evaluation, and critical thought about the given topic
.
    ◦ Teamwork and Decision-Making: Strengthens collaboration and decision-making skills by weighing opinions and aiming for consensus
.
• Personality Traits Evaluated for Individual Contribution: Participants are assessed on various traits demonstrating their potential effectiveness
:
    1. Reasoning Ability: Demonstrating analytical and logical skills
.
    2. Leadership: Capacity to influence or guide the group
.
    3. Openness: Willingness to be receptive to alternative perspectives
.
    4. Assertiveness: Confidently expressing one's point of view
.
    5. Initiative: Taking the lead in opening or steering the discussion
.
    6. Motivation: Displaying drive toward the discussion’s objective
.
    7. Attentive Listening: Actively processing and responding constructively to others’ inputs
.
    8. Awareness: Possessing sufficient knowledge relevant to the topic
.
• Techniques for Individual Contribution:
    ◦ Active Listening: To demonstrate attentiveness
, techniques should be employed, such as formulating clarifying questions or paraphrasing the speaker’s message to show comprehension,
.
    ◦ Structuring Contributions (Clarity): Contributions should be organized clearly, using specific verbal cues similar to those in formal explanation
,
:
        ▪ Signposts: Statements that indicate the structure and direction of the argument
.
        ▪ Frames: Statements that indicate the beginning and end of a subtopic within the discussion
.
        ▪ Foci: Statements or emphases used to highlight the key points of the argument
.
• Topic Analysis: This preparatory skill, related directly to the required trait of Awareness
, involves analyzing topics into their main parts or ‘keys’ and establishing the links between these parts. Effective analysis also determines the rules (if any) involved and specifies the kind of explanation required for the subject
.
3.4 Formal Presentation—Planning and Organizing
Careful and intelligent preparation is the key to a successful presentation
,, ensuring the performance is polished and professional. Preparation must focus on purpose, audience, structure, and aids
.
I. Planning (The Preparation Stage)
1. Define Purpose Determine the primary aim of the presentation, which can be
,,
:
• To Inform: To provide facts, data, stories, descriptions, or explanations, often requiring the speaker to put data into context or patterns
,
.
• To Persuade or Influence: To convince others or motivate action
,,
.
• To Entertain: To engage the audience playfully
,
.
• To Interact: To engage in dialogue with the audience
,
.
2. Audience Analysis The preparation must consider the audience's characteristics and needs
,
:
• Knowledge and Ability: Assess their theoretical/practical knowledge, education level, intelligence, and the terminology they are confident handling
,
.
• Needs and Expectations: Determine their expectations and needs; failure to satisfy these may lead the audience to feel their time was wasted
.
• Style and Tone: The language and tone of the presentation must be chosen to suit the audience
,
.
3. Organizing Structure and Content This pillar covers content selection, priority setting, and logical ordering
.
• Content Selection and Time Management: It is best to underestimate how much material can be covered in the time available, as being overambitious risks overwhelming the audience
.
• Setting Priorities: Select a small number of key points (priorities) that are absolutely essential for the listeners to grasp and remember
,. Highly detailed material should typically be placed in appendices or distributed in a handout
.
• Ordering Material: The sequence should be determined by
:
    ◦ The logic of the subject matter.
    ◦ The logic of learning: moving from the known to the unknown and from the easy to the difficult
.
    ◦ The need for variety (occasionally breaking strict logical order to keep engagement)
.
4. Preparation Techniques (The Lecture Preparation Model): A detailed approach to preparation includes:
• Free Association: Write down all facts, ideas, and questions related to the topic, then ring those you plan to use
.
• Working Title and Structure: Use the selected ideas to define the objectives and rough structure of the presentation
.
• Reading: Read only for specific ideas and facts; avoid excessive reading, as it can become a delaying tactic
.
• Writing Style: Crucially, write the presentation as you would speak it, rather than as you would write an academic article
.
II. Delivery and Structuring Moves
• Delivery Aids: Use prompt cards or notes to follow the pre-planned line of argument without reading directly from a script
. If using slides (e.g., PowerPoint), avoid excessive information on any single slide,. Reading directly from a prepared text prevents eye contact and inhibits awareness of the audience's response
.
• Verbal Structuring Moves (High Clarity): To help the audience follow complex ideas, presenters should employ verbal cues
,
:
    ◦ Signposts: Statements indicating the structure and direction of the explanation
.
    ◦ Frames: Statements marking the beginning and end of a subtopic, particularly important in complex explanations
.
    ◦ Foci: Statements and emphasis used to highlight key points, such as saying, “Now this is very important…”
.
    ◦ Links: Phrases connecting different parts of the argument
.
• Generating Interest: Expressiveness, supported by the judicious use of examples, a narrative mode of explaining, and the stimulation of curiosity, generates interest and raises levels of arousal and attention
.
• Handouts: If using handouts, try to delay distributing them until the end of the presentation, as people will want to read displayed text immediately
.
