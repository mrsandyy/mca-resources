UNIT-4 Different Forms of Writing Module
4.1 Résumé and Cover Letter
A. Résumé (Curriculum Vitae or CV)
A résumé is a written document that offers a concise summary of an individual's education, work experience, skills, and accomplishments
. It is typically used as a tool for job applications to showcase a candidate's qualifications and suitability for a position
.
Purpose and Benefits:
1. Introduction: It acts like a story about the candidate, introducing who they are, what they have done, and their proficiencies
.
2. Highlighting Strengths: It showcases the candidate's best skills, education, and achievements
.
3. Efficiency: It makes the application process easier for employers by allowing them to quickly assess if the candidate is a good fit
.
4. Seriousness: Having a résumé shows the employer that the candidate is serious about the job
.
Preparation: Researching Yourself Thorough preparation is the key to effective job applications
. Before writing the CV, analyze yourself across four areas
:
1. Experience: Include education, professional training, periods of employment (including part-time jobs), foreign travel, and voluntary work
.
2. Interests: List leisure time interests that entail useful skills relevant to the job
.
3. Skills: Include technical and professional skills, as well as personal skills (e.g., organizing events, training staff, chairing meetings)
.
4. Personal Qualities: List positive traits such as adaptability, commitment, meticulousness, or self-confidence
.

Content Structure (Format)
: A selling résumé is a prospectus setting out the applicant's virtues with supporting details
.
1. Contact Information: Full Name, Phone Number, Email Address, and optionally LinkedIn Profile or Home Address
.
2. Professional Summary or Objective: A brief statement highlighting career goals, skills, and value
.
3. Work Experience: List professional experiences in reverse chronological order
. Use bullet points to describe key responsibilities and achievements, quantifying results using metrics and numbers whenever possible
.
4. Education: Detail educational background, including institutions, degrees, dates, and honors/awards
.
5. Skills: Highlight skills relevant to the job, including both technical and soft skills
.
6. Achievements or Accomplishments: Specific accomplishments, awards, or recognition
.
7. Certifications and Training: List relevant certifications or additional programs completed
.
8. Projects (if applicable): Detail relevant projects, especially in fields that emphasize project-based work
.
9. Languages (if applicable): Mention proficiency in relevant languages
.
10. Referees (References): Names of at least two people (one character and one professional/work reference) who will provide references
.
Formatting Guidelines:
• A CV should be clear and uncluttered, leading the eye to key features
.
• Try to limit the CV to one side of A4, and certainly ensure all important information is on the first side. Never exceed two sides of A4
.
• The final draft should be word-processed and printed on high-quality paper, with care taken in choosing layout and typefaces
.
B. Cover Letter (Application Letter)
The cover letter accompanies the CV and forms a complete package
. It is an important selling tool
.
Purposes of the Cover Letter
:
1. Selling Tool: Points out key features of the CV and explains how skills and experience fit the particular position.
2. Personalization: Allows inclusion of material not in the CV, especially personal qualities.
3. Demonstration of Skill: Shows the employer the applicant's ability to communicate clearly and effectively in writing.
Structure (Format)
: The letter should follow the general guidelines for all business letters, comprising an introduction, a body, and a conclusion
.
1. Introduction: Detail the job being applied for and the circumstances that led to the application (e.g., advertisement, agency recommendation)
.
2. Body (1-3 punchy paragraphs): Presents the applicant's case to the employer
.
    ◦ Explain the attraction of the job and why the applicant is suitable.
    ◦ Emphasize skills and list personal qualities that would benefit the job.
    ◦ Answer any specific questions posed by the advertisement.
3. Conclusion: Should be brief, round the letter off, and leave the reader with a positive image
. It should sum up or spell out what the applicant would like to see happen (e.g., request an interview)
.
4.2 Abstract, Executive Summary, Mini Project Proposal
A. Abstract
An abstract is a form of condensation, similar to a précis or synopsis, used to reduce the length of a longer text
. In the context of a formal report, a sample abstract is provided as an example of content, typically found in the main body structure
.
B. Executive Summary
The Executive Summary is a crucial element in formal documentation, particularly reports
.
Purpose:
• The primary purpose is to set out the substance of the report briefly
.
• It allows busy readers to see at a glance whether the report is relevant and which aspects are of most interest
.
• It provides a rapid reference for those reading the report carefully
.
• It should contain the principal conclusions and recommendations
.
Format and Guidelines:
• It is listed second in the structure of a formal report, immediately after the Contents List
.
• Ideally, it should not exceed one side of a page; summaries that run too long can be self-defeating
.
C. Mini Project Proposal
A proposal is a written document that aims to promote a particular course of action
. It is openly biased, providing information and judgments used to further its own ends. Proposals, like reports, are formal written documents
.
Purpose and Importance:
• To convince a target audience (e.g., a publisher or client) that a specific plan is superior to alternatives
.
• If other possibilities are considered, it is only to show why they are incorrect and the favored one is right
.
Structure of Technical Proposals (General Format)
: While detailed contents may vary, proposals typically include a formalized structure, illustrated by samples that include:
• Title Page: Specifies the project name, recipient, and sender
.
• Executive Summary: A concise overview of the proposal's contents and recommendations
.
• Main Body: (Typically includes introduction, project description, methodology, expected results, schedule, and cost/funding details, based on related report structures
).
4.3 Short Essay Writing and Case Study
A. Short Essay Writing
An essay is an extended piece of prose used in the academic community to demonstrate and measure the writer’s grasp of a given subject
.
Preparation and Analysis: Effective writing begins with analyzing the question to ensure understanding of the required content and approach
. Questions may require different tasks
:
• Analyze: Examine a phenomenon and tease out significant features, describing and explaining their importance.
• Define: List the defining features of a term and support the definition with examples.
• Argue a Case: Examine two sides of an argument and evaluate them.
• Narrate: Tell a story (though writers must be cautious not to overuse narration when analysis is required)
.
Structuring the Essay
: The writing process includes generating ideas (lists, columns, web diagrams), research, and planning the order
. A cogent and coherent essay is built on:
1. Introduction: Should introduce the subject matter and indicate the approach to be taken, but avoid giving away the core argument immediately
.
2. Body: Must be composed of carefully constructed paragraphs
. Each paragraph should focus on one distinct topic and be linked seamlessly ("hooked") to the preceding and succeeding paragraphs
.
3. Conclusion: Should be short and effective
. It refers to the main points without being repetitive, ideally saving a final interesting point for emphasis
.
Academic Register and Style
: Academic writing adheres to specific conventions:
• Impersonal Tone: Avoid referring to 'me' and 'you'; generally use third-person constructions
.
• No Contractions: Avoid informal forms like 'won’t' and 'couldn’t'
.
• Avoid Slang: Avoid slang and excessive colloquialisms
.
• Evidence-Driven Argument: Argument must be logical and based on evidence, avoiding appeals to emotion
.
• Quotations and References: Short quotations go in the text with quotation marks; longer ones should be separated and indented. All must be properly attributed using a bibliography
.
B. Case Study
Case study material, along with detailed research data, is often placed in appendices of a formal report to avoid obscuring the main argument
. The academic context implies that case studies require description or exposition as modes of discourse
.
4.4 Business/Official Correspondence
Business correspondence includes all documents exchanged in written form, such as letters, circulars, memos, and emails
. Businesses often prefer written communication due to fewer chances of distortion
.
A. Business Letters
Business letters, though less common than email, establish conventions for structure and tone
. The letter's structure, layout, and conventions form a model, even for job applications
.
The Three-Part Structure (Format)
:
1. Introduction: States clearly the subject matter and purpose of the letter, enabling the recipient to decide how to handle it
. A heading after the salutation is one way to achieve this
.
2. Body: Develops the purpose step-by-step, explaining the argument or story. It is divided into paragraphs, each covering a separate, logically connected aspect
.
3. Conclusion: Re-emphasizes the purpose, often spelling out what action is desired
. It should be positive and unambiguous
.
Tone and Style: Tone should be suitable for the recipient, avoiding casual language, over-formality, pomposity, and jargon
.

The 7 Cs of Effective Business Communication
Effective correspondence must adhere to principles related to clarity, completeness, and courtesy
:
1. Clarity: Use short sentences (under 30 words, one idea per sentence)
, prefer verbs over long nouns, and avoid jargon and ambiguity
.
2. Completeness: Include all relevant facts (e.g., size, quantity, dates) and check the "five Ws and H" (who, what, where, when, why, and how)
.
3. Conciseness: Be brief, including only relevant facts and avoiding repetition or trite expressions
.
4. Consideration: Adopt the "you-attitude" (focusing on the reader's perspective) and emphasize positive facts, even when refusing a request
. Avoid gender bias
.
5. Correctness: Transmit correct facts in correct language
, send the message at the correct time (outdated information is useless), and use the correct style adapted to the receiver's needs (adaptability)
.
6. Courtesy: Answer promptly
. Omit irritating expressions (e.g., "you forgot"). If apologizing, do so sincerely
.
7. Concreteness: (Implied through using precise, familiar, and concrete words and avoiding wordiness)
.
B. Official Correspondence: Email, Memos, and Circulars
Email (E-writing): Email is a medium that allows communication cheaply and almost instantly
. Longer emails require a clear structure, similar to letters
.
• Key Elements (Topping and Tailing)
:
    ◦ Subject: A brief description for ease of reference
.
    ◦ To/Cc/Bcc: Specifies the recipients and whether copies are open or blind
.
    ◦ Greeting: Can range from formal ('Dear [Name]') to informal ('Hello' or 'Hi')
.
    ◦ Signature: Often contains business titles and contact details
.
• Attachments: Useful for sending documents that are longer than the email body or require specific formatting
.
• Etiquette: Avoid excessive use of capital letters (which may imply shouting)
and only use initialisms (e.g., FYI, ASAP) when the recipient is likely to appreciate them
.
Memorandum (Memo): An inter-office communication
. Memos are one of the types of written communication
.
Circulars: Written communication used in organizations, often for Downward Communication to announce policies and procedures
. Types include informative, public, partnership/company, and official circulars
.
