# UNIT-3: Speaking and Reading

## 3.1 Different Types of Reading

Effective reading involves selecting the appropriate strategy based on your purpose and the nature of the text.

### A. The Four Basic Types of Reading

1. **Skimming**
   
   - **Purpose:** To quickly gather the general sense or "gist" of a document.
   
   - **Method:** The eyes run quickly over the text, noting key words, concepts, and important information without reading every word.
   
   - **Application:** Rapidly reading a newspaper to identify interesting articles or previewing a report.

2. **Scanning**
   
   - **Purpose:** To locate a specific piece of information (e.g., a date, a name, a number).
   
   - **Method:** The eyes dart over the text looking *exclusively* for the specific target.
   
   - **Application:** Looking up a number in a directory, finding a time on a schedule, or searching an index.

3. **Extensive Reading**
   
   - **Purpose:** To obtain a general understanding of a subject or for enjoyment.
   
   - **Method:** Reading longer texts (novels, long articles) fluently without worrying about the exact meaning of every unknown word.
   
   - **Application:** Reading a novel for pleasure or monitoring business trends via general reports.

4. **Intensive Reading**
   
   - **Purpose:** To extract detailed, specific information; requires 100% comprehension.
   
   - **Method:** Close, accurate reading where every word and fact is scrutinized.
   
   - **Application:** Reading contracts, insurance claims, or textbooks to understand complex theories.

### B. Note Taking

Note taking acts as the bridge between consuming information and generating new ideas.

- **Purposes:**
  
  - **Memory:** Aids in recalling details that would otherwise be lost.
  
  - **Comprehension:** Converting information into your own words deepens understanding.
  
  - **Review:** Creates a resource for exam preparation.
  
  - **Planning:** Helps organize thoughts for writing longer texts.

- **Common Formats:**
  
  - **Freeform:** Good for quick idea collection.
  
  - **List:** Useful for hierarchical planning.
  
  - **Table:** Best for comparing categories of information.
  
  - **Flowchart:** Ideal for recording processes.
  
  - **Concept/Mind Map:** Flexible format starting with a central idea and branching out; excellent for showing relationships.

- **Tips for Success:**
  
  - **Simplify:** Use your own words; avoid long sentences.
  
  - **Speed:** Use abbreviations and symbols.
  
  - **Visuals:** Use colors, underlining, and sketches to emphasize key points.

### C. Paraphrasing

Paraphrasing is the skill of expressing someone else's ideas in your own words without changing the original meaning.

- **Why Paraphrase?**
  
  - **Avoid Plagiarism:** Essential when using research.
  
  - **Demonstrate Understanding:** Proves you have grasped the speaker/writer's message.
  
  - **Learning:** Reinforces retention of the material.

- **How to Paraphrase:**
  
  1. Read/listen until you fully understand the main idea.
  
  2. Break down complex sentences into smaller parts.
  
  3. Re-express the idea using different vocabulary and structure.
  
  4. Ensure the meaning remains identical to the source.

## 3.2 Comprehension Passage

Reading comprehension measures a reader's ability to process text, understand its meaning, and integrate it with what they already know.

### Methods for Deep Comprehension

1. **Intensive Reading:** As noted above, this is critical for inferring central ideas and interpreting significance.

2. **SQ3R Method:** A structured approach to "immaculate reading."
   
   - **S**urvey
   
   - **Q**uestion
   
   - **R**ead
   
   - **R**ecite
   
   - **R**eview

3. **Visualization:** Converting text into mental images to boost retention.

### Obstacles to Comprehension (Habits to Avoid)

- **Plodding:** Reading word-by-word mechanically, which slows processing and reduces overall understanding.

- **Vocalization:** Moving lips or reading aloud; this limits reading speed to speaking speed.

- **Regression:** Habitually jumping back to reread words, indicating a lack of confidence or focus.

- **Lack of Concentration:** Attempting to read while the mind is preoccupied.

## 3.3 Group Discussion (GD) Strategies

A Group Discussion is a formal methodology used to gauge a candidate's personality, communication skills, and knowledge.

### Techniques for Individual Contribution

To score well in a GD, you must demonstrate specific traits:

1. **Active Listening:**
   
   - Do not just wait for your turn to speak.
   
   - Paraphrase others' points to show you are listening ("As [Name] just pointed out...").
   
   - Ask clarifying questions to move the discussion forward.

2. **Structuring Contributions:** Use verbal "signposts" to make your arguments clear to the evaluators:
   
   - **Signposts:** "I have three points to make. First..."
   
   - **Frames:** "Moving away from the financial aspect, let's look at the social impact..."
   
   - **Foci:** "The most critical issue here is..."

3. **Topic Analysis:**
   
   - Before speaking, analyze the topic to identify "Keys" (main parts).
   
   - Establish links between these parts.
   
   - Determine if the topic requires a solution, an opinion, or an abstract analysis.

### Traits Evaluated

- **Reasoning Ability:** Logic and analysis.

- **Leadership:** Guiding the group without dominating.

- **Openness:** Accepting others' viewpoints.

- **Assertiveness:** Standing ground politely.

- **Initiative:** Starting the discussion or reviving it.

## 3.4 Formal Presentation: Planning and Organizing

Success in a presentation is 90% preparation and 10% delivery.

### I. Planning (The Preparation Stage)

**1. Define Purpose** What is the primary aim?

- **To Inform:** Teaching, reporting data.

- **To Persuade:** Selling an idea, motivating action.

- **To Entertain:** Engaging the audience.

- **To Interact:** Facilitating a workshop or dialogue.

**2. Audience Analysis** Tailor your content to them.

- **Knowledge:** What do they already know? (Don't bore them with basics; don't confuse them with jargon).

- **Needs:** What do they expect to gain?

- **Style/Tone:** Formal vs. informal based on the crowd.

**3. Organizing Structure**

- **Select Content:** Be ruthless. It is better to cover a few points well than many points poorly.

- **Prioritize:** Identify "Must Know" vs. "Nice to Know."

- **Order:** Move from Known → Unknown, or Easy → Difficult.

**4. Preparation Model**

- **Free Association:** Brainstorm everything.

- **Structure:** Create a working title and rough outline.

- **Write for the Ear:** Write as you speak, not as you write an essay.

### II. Delivery and Structuring Moves

**Verbal Cues (The "Glue" of the presentation):**

- **Signposts:** "Next, I will discuss..."

- **Frames:** "That concludes the first section. Now..."

- **Foci:** "This is the most important slide..."

- **Links:** Connecting the current point to the previous one.

**Delivery Aids:**

- Use prompt cards, not a full script (reading destroys eye contact).

- Slides should have minimal text.

- Distribute handouts *after* the presentation to prevent distraction.



# UNIT-4: Different Forms of Writing

## 4.1 Résumé and Cover Letter

### A. Résumé (Curriculum Vitae or CV)

A résumé is a written document offering a concise summary of an individual's education, work experience, skills, and accomplishments. It serves as a marketing tool to showcase a candidate's qualifications to potential employers.

**Purpose and Benefits:**

- **Introduction:** Acts as a "story" about the candidate, introducing who they are and what they have done.

- **Highlighting Strengths:** Focuses on the candidate's best skills and achievements.

- **Efficiency:** Allows employers to quickly assess if a candidate is a good fit.

- **Seriousness:** Demonstrates professional commitment to the job application.

**Preparation (Researching Yourself):** Before writing, analyze yourself in four key areas:

1. **Experience:** Education, professional training, employment (including part-time), and voluntary work.

2. **Interests:** Leisure activities that suggest useful skills relevant to the job.

3. **Skills:** Technical, professional, and personal skills (e.g., organization, leadership).

4. **Personal Qualities:** Traits like adaptability, commitment, and meticulousness.

**Types of Résumés:**

| Type              | Focus/Structure                                                           | Advantages                                        | Disadvantages                                                       |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Chronological** | Lists experience in reverse chronological order (most recent first).      | Highlights career progression and continuity.     | Shows gaps in employment; may not highlight specific skills easily. |
| **Functional**    | Groups skills and qualifications under headings rather than by date.      | Focuses on strengths; hides gaps or inexperience. | Harder to see career history or continuity.                         |
| **Combination**   | Combines functional skill highlighting with a chronological work history. | Provides a comprehensive overview.                | Can be longer or more complex to format.                            |

**Content Structure (The "Selling" Résumé):**

1. **Contact Information:** Name, phone, email, LinkedIn.

2. **Professional Summary:** Brief statement of goals and value.

3. **Work Experience:** Reverse chronological list with quantified achievements (metrics/numbers).

4. **Education:** Institutions, degrees, dates, honors.

5. **Skills:** Relevant technical and soft skills.

6. **Achievements:** Awards or specific recognition.

7. **Certifications:** Additional training completed.

8. **Projects:** Relevant project work.

9. **Languages:** Proficiency levels.

10. **Referees:** Professional and character references.

**Formatting Guidelines:**

- Clear and uncluttered layout.

- Limit to 1-2 pages (A4).

- High-quality presentation (font, layout).

### B. Cover Letter (Application Letter)

A cover letter accompanies the CV to create a complete application package.

**Purpose:**

- **Selling Tool:** Connects the CV's skills to the specific job requirements.

- **Personalization:** Adds personal qualities and context not found in the CV.

- **Demonstration:** Proves the applicant’s ability to communicate clearly in writing.

**Structure:**

1. **Introduction:** States the job applied for and the source of the advertisement/referral.

2. **Body (1-3 paragraphs):**
   
   - Explains interest in the role.
   
   - Matches skills/qualities to the job description.
   
   - Answers specific questions from the ad.

3. **Conclusion:** Brief wrap-up, positive tone, and call to action (e.g., requesting an interview).

## 4.2 Abstract, Executive Summary, Mini Project Proposal

### A. Abstract

- **Definition:** A condensation or synopsis used to reduce a long text into a brief summary.

- **Usage:** Typically found at the beginning of formal reports or academic papers to give a preview of the content.

### B. Executive Summary

A crucial element of formal reports designed for busy decision-makers.

- **Purpose:** To set out the substance of the report briefly, allowing readers to quickly determine relevance and see principal conclusions/recommendations.

- **Format:** Listed second in a report (after Contents). Ideally limits length to one page to avoid defeating its purpose.

### C. Mini Project Proposal

A written document aiming to promote a specific course of action or plan.

- **Purpose:** To convince a target audience (client/publisher) that a specific plan is superior to alternatives. It is openly "biased" towards the proposed solution.

- **Structure:**
  
  - **Title Page:** Project name, sender, recipient.
  
  - **Executive Summary:** Concise overview of the proposal.
  
  - **Main Body:** Introduction, description, methodology, expected results, schedule, and costs.

## 4.3 Short Essay Writing and Case Study

### A. Short Essay Writing

An extended piece of prose used to demonstrate a writer’s grasp of a subject.

**Preparation & Analysis:** Understand the question type:

- **Analyze:** Examine features and explain importance.

- **Define:** List features and provide examples.

- **Argue:** Evaluate two sides of a case.

- **Narrate:** Tell a story (use sparingly in analysis).

**Structure:**

1. **Introduction:** Introduces subject and approach without revealing the full argument immediately.

2. **Body:** Distinct paragraphs, each focusing on one topic, linked ("hooked") seamlessly to the next.

3. **Conclusion:** Short, effective summary of main points without simple repetition.

**Academic Style:**

- **Impersonal:** Use third-person (avoid "I", "me", "you").

- **Formal:** No contractions ("won't" $\rightarrow$ "will not").

- **No Slang:** Avoid colloquialisms.

- **Evidence-Based:** Logic over emotion.

- **Citations:** Proper attribution of quotes and references.

### B. Case Study

- **Definition:** Detailed research data or descriptions of specific scenarios.

- **Placement:** Often placed in **Appendices** of formal reports to avoid cluttering the main text.

- **Mode:** Requires description or exposition.

## 4.4 Business / Official Correspondence

### A. Business Letters

Standard formal communication adhering to specific conventions.

**Structure:**

1. **Introduction:** Clearly states subject and purpose.

2. **Body:** Step-by-step development of the argument/message in logical paragraphs.

3. **Conclusion:** Positive, unambiguous statement of desired action.

**Tone:**

- Avoid passive voice ("We sent" rather than "It was sent").

- Avoid jargon; use simple, concrete words.

- Use short sentences.

**Greetings and Endings:**

- **Formal (Unknown):** *Dear Sir or Madam* / *Yours faithfully*

- **Formal (Known Name):** *Dear Mr. Smith* / *Yours sincerely*

- **Semi-Formal:** *Dear John* / *Yours sincerely*

**The 7 Cs of Effective Communication:**

1. **Clarity:** Short sentences, no ambiguity.

2. **Completeness:** Includes all facts (Who, What, Where, When, Why, How).

3. **Conciseness:** Brief, relevant, no repetition.

4. **Consideration:** "You-attitude" (focus on reader), positive emphasis.

5. **Correctness:** Accurate facts, language, and timing.

6. **Courtesy:** Prompt answers, sincere apologies, polite language.

7. **Concreteness:** Precise and specific wording.

### B. Official Correspondence (Email, Memos, Circulars)

**Email (E-writing):**

- **Nature:** Cheap, instant, requires structure for longer messages.

- **Key Elements:**
  
  - **Subject:** Brief and descriptive.
  
  - **To/Cc/Bcc:** Proper addressing.
  
  - **Greeting/Signature:** Appropriate formality level.

- **Etiquette:** No excessive caps (shouting), use initialisms (FYI, ASAP) only if understood.

**Memorandum (Memo):**

- Internal inter-office communication used for brief updates or requests within an organization.

**Circulars:**

- Used for **downward communication** to announce policies or procedures to a large group (e.g., public, partnership, or official circulars).
