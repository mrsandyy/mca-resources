# UNIT-3: Speaking and Reading

## 3.1 Different Types of Reading

Effective reading is not a one-size-fits-all process; it involves consciously selecting the appropriate strategy based on your specific purpose and the nature of the text. A skilled reader switches between these modes seamlessly.

### A. The Four Basic Types of Reading

1. **Skimming**
   
   - **Purpose:** To quickly gather the general sense, central theme, or "gist" of a document without getting bogged down in details.
   
   - **Method:** The eyes run quickly over the text, focusing on structural cues like headings, subheadings, the first and last paragraphs, and topic sentences. The reader intentionally skips supporting details and examples.
   
   - **Application:** Rapidly reading a newspaper to decide which articles are worth your time, previewing a business report before a meeting, or reviewing a syllabus to understand the course scope.

2. **Scanning**
   
   - **Purpose:** To locate a specific, predetermined piece of information (e.g., a date, a name, a statistical figure) without processing the surrounding context.
   
   - **Method:** The eyes dart over the text in a zigzag or vertical pattern, looking *exclusively* for the specific target or keyword. This is similar to using the "Ctrl+F" function in a digital document; your brain ignores everything that isn't the target data.
   
   - **Application:** Looking up a phone number in a directory, finding a flight time on a departure board, locating a specific term in an index, or checking a price in a catalog.

3. **Extensive Reading**
   
   - **Purpose:** To obtain a general, broad understanding of a subject or simply for enjoyment and fluency building.
   
   - **Method:** Reading longer texts (novels, long articles, biographies) fluently and at a comfortable pace. The focus is on the overall narrative or argument rather than minute details. Readers should avoid stopping to look up every unknown word, inferring meaning from context instead to maintain flow.
   
   - **Application:** Reading a novel for pleasure, browsing magazines, or monitoring general business trends via industry reports to stay informed without needing to memorize data.

4. **Intensive Reading**
   
   - **Purpose:** To extract detailed, specific information and achieve deep understanding; this requires close to 100% comprehension.
   
   - **Method:** Close, accurate reading where every word, fact, and grammatical structure is scrutinized. This often involves re-reading, annotating, and pausing to reflect. It is a slower, more energy-intensive process.
   
   - **Application:** Reading legal contracts before signing, analyzing insurance claims, studying complex academic textbooks, or following safety instructions for hazardous equipment.

### B. Note Taking

Note taking acts as the critical bridge between passively consuming information and actively generating new ideas. It forces the brain to process information rather than just letting it pass through.

- **Purposes:**
  
  - **Memory:** Human memory is fallible; notes aid in recalling specific details, statistics, and arguments that would otherwise fade within hours.
  
  - **Comprehension:** The act of translating information into your own words (encoding) forces you to understand the logic and hierarchy of the content.
  
  - **Review:** Creates a condensed, personalized resource for exam preparation, saving you from re-reading entire textbooks.
  
  - **Planning:** Notes provide the raw material needed to organize thoughts for writing essays, reports, or preparing presentations.
  
  - **Generating Ideas:** The physical act of writing can trigger 'brainstorming,' leading to new connections and insights.

- **Common Formats:**
  
  - **Freeform:** Writing continuously with headings and bullet points. Good for quick idea collection during fast-paced lectures.
  
  - **List:** Uses indentation to show hierarchy (Main point -> Sub-point -> Detail). Useful for structured planning.
  
  - **Table:** Arranges data in rows and columns. Best for comparing items (e.g., Pros vs. Cons) or categorizing information.
  
  - **Flowchart:** Uses boxes and arrows to show sequence. Ideal for recording processes, algorithms, or historical timelines.
  
  - **Concept/Mind Map:** A flexible, non-linear format starting with a central idea and branching out. Excellent for visual learners to show relationships between disparate ideas.

- **Tips for Effective Note-Taking:**
  
  - **Clarity and Simplicity:** Always write in your own words. Avoid transcribing the speaker verbatim; paraphrase to ensure understanding.
  
  - **Speed:** Develop your own shorthand (e.g., "w/" for with, "&" for and, "b/c" for because) to keep up with the flow of information.
  
  - **Visual Aids:** Use simple diagrams, arrows, or symbols to represent connections if words are too slow or complex.
  
  - **Structure/Emphasis:** Use highlighters or different ink colors to make headings and key terms stand out for easy scanning later.

### C. Paraphrasing

Paraphrasing is the advanced skill of expressing someone else's ideas in your own words and style without altering the original meaning or tone.

- **Why Paraphrase?**
  
  - **Avoid Plagiarism:** It is essential when incorporating research into your work to distinguish your voice from your sources while giving credit.
  
  - **Demonstrate Understanding:** You cannot paraphrase what you do not understand. Successful paraphrasing proves you have grasped the core message.
  
  - **Learning:** The mental effort required to paraphrase reinforces long-term retention of the material.

- **How to Paraphrase:**
  
  1. **Deep Listening/Reading:** Read or listen until you fully understand the main idea and the nuances.
  
  2. **Deconstruct:** Break down complex, compound sentences into smaller, manageable conceptual units.
  
  3. **Reconstruct:** Re-express the idea using synonyms, different sentence structures (e.g., changing active voice to passive), and your own unique phrasing.
  
  4. **Verify:** Compare your version with the original to ensure the meaning remains identical and no accidental copying occurred.

## 3.2 Comprehension Passage

Reading comprehension is more than just recognizing words; it measures a reader's ability to process text, understand its deeper meaning, and integrate it with existing knowledge.

### Methods for Deep Comprehension

1. **Intensive Reading:** As noted above, this is critical for inferring central ideas, interpreting the author's tone, and understanding the significance of the arguments presented.

2. **SQ3R Method:** A structured, five-step approach to "immaculate reading" that ensures material is retained.
   
   - **S**urvey: Glance through headings, charts, and summaries to get an overview.
   
   - **Q**uestion: Turn headings into questions (e.g., "Causes of War" becomes "What were the causes of war?") to guide your reading.
   
   - **R**ead: Read the section actively to find the answers to your questions.
   
   - **R**ecite: Look away from the text and try to answer the questions in your own words.
   
   - **R**eview: Go back over the material to verify your answers and fix them in your memory.

3. **Visualization:** Converting abstract text into mental images or "movies" in your mind to boost engagement and retention.

### Obstacles to Comprehension (Habits to Avoid)

- **Plodding:** Reading word-by-word mechanically. This fragments meaning and slows processing, making it harder to grasp the overall sentence structure.

- **Vocalization:** Moving lips or sub-vocalizing (reading aloud in your head). This limits reading speed to your speaking speed (approx. 150 wpm) rather than your brain's processing speed.

- **Regression:** Habitually jumping back to reread words or phrases. This indicates a lack of focus or confidence and breaks the rhythm of understanding.

- **Lack of Concentration:** Attempting to read while the mind is preoccupied with other worries or distractions, rendering the time spent useless.

## 3.3 Group Discussion (GD) Strategies

A Group Discussion (GD) is a formal methodology used in academic and professional settings to gauge a candidate's personality, communication skills, leadership potential, and knowledge base.

### Techniques for Individual Contribution

To score well in a GD, you must demonstrate constructive traits rather than just dominance:

1. **Active Listening:**
   
   - Listening is as important as speaking. Do not just wait for your turn; process what others say.
   
   - **Paraphrasing:** Summarize others' points to show you value their input ("As [Name] just pointed out regarding the cost...") before adding your own.
   
   - **Clarifying:** Ask questions to clear up ambiguities ("Do you mean X or Y?"), which helps move the discussion forward.

2. **Structuring Contributions:** Use verbal "signposts" to make your arguments logical and easy for evaluators to follow:
   
   - **Signposts:** Guide the listener through your logic (e.g., "I have three points to make. First... Secondly... Finally...").
   
   - **Frames:** Mark boundaries between topics (e.g., "Moving away from the financial aspect, let's look at the social impact...").
   
   - **Foci:** Highlight the most significant part of your argument (e.g., "The *crucial* issue here is not cost, but safety...").

3. **Topic Analysis:**
   
   - Before speaking, mentally dissect the topic to identify "Keys" (main components or stakeholders).
   
   - Establish links between these parts (cause and effect).
   
   - Determine the nature of the topic: Does it require a specific solution (Case Study), an opinion (Abstract), or a balanced analysis (Controversial)?

### Traits Evaluated

- **Reasoning Ability:** The ability to think logically and support arguments with facts rather than emotions.

- **Leadership:** The capacity to guide the group, manage conflict, and ensure everyone gets a chance to speak—not just dominating the conversation.

- **Openness:** The flexibility to accept valid counter-arguments and modify your stance if necessary.

- **Assertiveness:** The ability to state your point firmly and confidently without being aggressive or rude.

- **Initiative:** Taking the lead in starting the discussion or reviving it when silence falls.

## 3.4 Formal Presentation: Planning and Organizing

A successful presentation is the result of 90% preparation and only 10% delivery. Professionalism comes from careful planning.

### I. Planning (The Preparation Stage)

**1. Define Purpose** What is the specific goal?

- **To Inform:** Teaching, reporting data, or explaining a process. (Focus on clarity).

- **To Persuade:** Selling an idea, convincing an audience to change their behavior, or motivating action. (Focus on benefits).

- **To Entertain:** Engaging the audience playfully (e.g., after-dinner speech).

- **To Interact:** Facilitating a workshop or dialogue where audience participation is key.

**2. Audience Analysis** A presentation must be customer-centric.

- **Knowledge:** Assess their background. Don't bore experts with basics, and don't confuse beginners with undefined jargon.

- **Needs/Expectations:** Why are they here? What problem do they need you to solve? Failing to address this makes them feel their time was wasted.

- **Style/Tone:** Adjust your language (formal vs. informal) and attire based on the audience's culture.

**3. Organizing Structure**

- **Select Content:** Be ruthless in editing. It is better to cover three points thoroughly than ten points superficially. Overloading the audience leads to cognitive fatigue.

- **Prioritize:** Distinguish between "Must Know" (core message), "Should Know" (supporting details), and "Nice to Know" (trivia).

- **Order:** Structure the flow logically:
  
  - Logical/Chronological order.
  
  - Known → Unknown (Start with what they understand).
  
  - Easy → Difficult (Build complexity gradually).

**4. Preparation Techniques**

- **Free Association:** Brainstorm every fact, idea, and story related to the topic without filtering.

- **Structure:** Group these ideas to define objectives and create a rough outline.

- **Write for the Ear:** Spoken language differs from written language. Use shorter sentences, active verbs, and conversational transitions. Do not write an academic essay.

### II. Delivery and Structuring Moves

**Verbal Cues (The "Glue" of the presentation):** Complex ideas need signposting to keep the audience on track.

- **Signposts:** "Next, I will discuss the three main causes..."

- **Frames:** "That concludes the financial overview. Now, let's move to marketing..."

- **Foci:** "If you remember only one thing from this slide, it should be this..."

- **Links:** "This connects back to our earlier point about..."

**Delivery Aids:**

- **Prompt Cards:** Use small cards with keywords. Never read from a full script, as it destroys eye contact and makes you sound robotic.

- **Visuals:** Slides should be visual aids, not teleprompters. Avoid "walls of text."

- **Handouts:** Distribute these *after* the presentation. If you give them out early, the audience will read them instead of listening to you.
